# General Principles of Fracture Healing

## Introduction

A fracture is a break in the continuity of bone. Healing is a repair process that
restores the bone's structural integrity through a sequence of overlapping
stages, driven by the periosteum, the surrounding soft tissues, and the local
blood supply.

Recovery is not simply "bone regrowing" — it depends on **stability**, **blood
supply**, and **apposition of the fragments**. Interfragmentary strain above
roughly 2% favours cartilaginous repair, while near-rigid fixation permits
direct Haversian remodelling.

## Modules

### Stages of Healing

**Immediate response**

- Haematoma forms in the fracture gap.
  - Occurs within hours of injury.
  - Fibrin mesh provides the initial scaffold.
- Local vessels are torn, producing a zone of **hypoxia** and necrosis.

**Inflammatory phase**

- **Initiating event:** bone and periosteal disruption.
- **Sequence:** haematoma → inflammation → granulation tissue.
- **Outcome:** a soft procallus that splints the fragments.
- Repair is driven by `TGF-β`, `BMP-2` and `PDGF` signalling.

### Factors Affecting Healing

- **Systemic factors**
  - Age — children heal faster than adults.
  - Nutrition — calcium, vitamin D and protein availability.
  - Smoking — impairs revascularisation.
- **Local factors**
  - Blood supply to the fragments.
  - Degree of soft-tissue injury.
  - Infection and interposition of tissue.

### Comparison of Repair Types

| Feature | Primary (direct) | Secondary (indirect) |
| --- | :---: | ---: |
| Callus | Absent | Prominent |
| Stability required | Absolute | Relative |
| Speed of union | Slower to begin | Faster initially |

> **Key point:** rigid fixation does not speed union; it changes the *mechanism*.
